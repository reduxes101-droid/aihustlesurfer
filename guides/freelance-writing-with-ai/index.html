Freelance writing with AI without producing something a client can smell

Clients are not against AI. They are against being sent a first draft. The workflow experienced freelancers describe for keeping rates steady while raising output, and the rules that protect the relationship.

GuideFreelance writing with AI without producing something a client can smellTop

Freelance writing is the fastest route to actual income with AI tools, and the one where a bad workflow does the most damage. Send a client something that reads like a chatbot and you lose the client. Refuse to use the tools and you lose to the writer who charges the same and delivers twice as much.

This is the process that working freelancers most consistently describe, assembled from published workflows, community threads and the tools' own documentation.

The rule that governs everything

Nothing goes to a client that you would be embarrassed to defend line by line.

That rule means every draft gets read, and most of it gets changed. If you are not willing to do that, the tools will not save your business. They will end it faster.

Setup: one Project per client

Claude Projects are the usual choice. ChatGPT's custom instructions and memory can do a version of this, and the side-by-side video covers why writers tend to prefer Claude for this specific job.

Each client Project should contain:

  • Their style guide if they have one, or three paragraphs describing the voice if they do not.
  • Two or three pieces of theirs that they like. Real ones, not ideals.
  • A list of banned phrases and structures. A good starting list: no rhetorical questions in intros, no "in today's fast-paced world", no three-item lists with the third item as a twist, no closing summary paragraph.
  • Any product facts the client has confirmed, so the draft does not invent features.

Setup takes about twenty minutes per client and saves that on every piece afterwards.

Research before drafting

The tools will write confidently about things that are not true. For anything with facts in it, research happens first, in Perplexity, and every source behind a claim you will make gets opened and read. Notes go into the draft prompt. The model is told to use only those facts.

This is the step most people skip and the step that produces the corrections email from the client.

Drafting

The brief goes into the Project as the client wrote it, plus your angle, plus the notes. Ask for the draft at the exact word count. Then two passes:

  1. "Cut anything a reader could have predicted. Keep the argument."
  2. "Flag any sentence where you are not certain of the fact."

The second pass is the one worth stealing. The model will tell you where it was guessing, if you ask.

The human pass

This is where the time goes and where the money is earned. In order:

  1. Rewrite the first paragraph from scratch. AI intros are the most recognisable part of AI writing.
  2. Read it aloud. Anything you stumble over, the reader will too. Anything you would not say, cut.
  3. Check every number, name and claim against the notes. Even after the flagging pass.
  4. Add one thing the model could not know. A specific example from the client's business, a detail from a call, an opinion. This is what makes the piece theirs.
  5. Delete the last paragraph if it summarises. It usually does.

Freelancers who track this put the human pass at thirty to forty-five minutes for a 1,200-word post, against three to four hours for the whole piece before AI. The productivity gain is all in the drafting, not the thinking.

Should you tell the client?

The disclosure that experienced freelancers recommend is the plain one: "I use AI tools for research and first drafts. Everything you receive is edited and fact-checked by me, and I stand behind every line." The common report is that clients rarely object, often assumed as much, and sometimes ask for help doing the same in-house, which becomes paid work.

Hiding it is a bad bet. Clients increasingly run detection tools, which are unreliable, and increasingly recognise the patterns themselves, which is reliable. Being upfront removes the risk and positions you as someone who knows how to use the tools well.

Pricing

Do not lower your rates because the draft was faster. The client is paying for the finished piece and the judgement in it. If you charge per word, consider moving to per piece or per project; it removes the conversation about how long it took.

Where rates have fallen is at the bottom: content-mill work at cents per word. That work is gone, and it was never a business.

Where to find the work

The same places as always. Job boards for writers, direct outreach to businesses whose blogs have gone quiet, referrals from existing clients. AI has not changed this part at all, which disappoints people who hoped the tool would do the selling.

What has changed is the offer. "I can deliver four quality pieces a month at this rate" is a stronger pitch than it was two years ago, because it is now deliverable.

Tools, priced

  • Claude Pro, $20. Essential.
  • Perplexity, free tier is enough for most writers. Pro at $20 for daily research.
  • ChatGPT, free tier for images and the odd second opinion.
  • Jasper: reviewed here and not recommended for a solo writer. The review says why.

Total: $20 a month for a workflow that can double output. There are not many purchases like that.
