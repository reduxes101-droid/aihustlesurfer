There is a genre of video where someone shows a dashboard, says a number, and lists seven tools. This guide is the opposite of that. It draws on what freelancers, channel operators and small publishers consistently report across forums, case studies and platform documentation, and it sorts the methods by how often they hold up.

The short version: AI tools make an existing skill faster. They do not replace the skill, and they do not replace the part where you find someone willing to pay. Every method below that tends to work is a normal way to earn online, made cheaper or quicker by a tool. Every method that tends to fail is one where the tool was supposed to be the business.

What tends to work

1. Freelance writing, with AI as a drafting tool

This is the most reliable income on the list and the least exciting. Clients pay for blog posts, product descriptions, email sequences and web copy. The tools do the first draft; the writer does the thinking, the editing and the client relationship.

What changed with AI, according to working writers, is throughput. Someone who could deliver two solid articles a day can deliver three or four at the same quality, if they are honest about the editing pass. Rates at the top of the market have held up better than predicted, because those clients pay for judgement rather than typing. The bottom of the market, content-mill work at cents per word, has largely disappeared, and it was never a business.

Tools: Claude for drafts and editing, Perplexity for research. The freelance writing guide covers the workflow.

2. A faceless YouTube channel, treated as a job

YouTube's Partner Program requires 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 public watch hours in a year or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days before a channel earns from ads. Operators who share their timelines typically describe six months to over a year of consistent publishing to get there, and modest earnings at first: enough to cover tools, not to live on. That is the normal shape of it.

What separates the channels that get there, in their own accounts, is not the AI. It is a niche with advertiser demand, scripts with an actual point, and tight editing. The AI reduces the cost of each video from a full day to an hour or two, which is what makes a three-a-week schedule possible for one person.

Tools: ElevenLabs for the voice, Descript for the edit, Runway for some B-roll. The full guide walks through it.

3. Pinterest traffic to a site that earns

Pinterest describes itself as a visual discovery engine rather than a social network, and it behaves like one: pins surface in search for months, and a new account with no followers can get reach in weeks. It has no policy against generated images. Publishers who use it report it as one of the few remaining sources of free traffic to blogs.

The AI part is pin production. Generating backgrounds in Midjourney and finishing in Canva turns pin creation from an hour a day into an hour a week. The content the pins point at still has to be worth reading. See the Pinterest guide.

4. Small automation and setup work for other businesses

This one gets less attention than it deserves. Local businesses and solo operators will pay to have their admin automated: leads into a CRM, invoices chased, a first reply drafted for review. It is Zapier and a few hours of thought, and freelancers who offer it often describe it turning into a monthly retainer.

It works because most people do not want to learn the tool. If you are willing to, and you can explain what you built in plain language, there is steady work here. It requires talking to people, which is why fewer creators do it.

5. Digital products, if you already have an audience

Printable planners, templates, small ebooks. AI makes producing them fast: Canva for the design, Claude for the text. Sellers on Etsy and similar marketplaces report small, steady income from listings that need no ongoing work.

The catch is distribution. Without an audience or a marketplace with search traffic, a product nobody knows about earns nothing, however quickly it was made. Note that Etsy now requires sellers to disclose when AI was used in creating a listing. This sits at number five because the product is the easy part.

What tends not to work

Selling AI-generated art as art

Marketplaces are flooded, buyers can often tell, and platforms increasingly label or restrict it. Sellers who report on this describe listings that get views and almost no sales. The images may be good. The market for them is not there.

AI-written ebooks on Amazon

Amazon KDP requires authors to declare AI-generated content and has limited the number of titles an account can publish per day, in direct response to the flood. Search results in most non-fiction categories are crowded with near-identical books, and the ones that sell have a person's name and reputation behind them. Without that, the book is invisible, and self-publishers who tried the volume approach say so consistently.

Faceless channels in niches chosen by a keyword tool

A common pattern in creator post-mortems: a niche was picked because a tool rated it low competition, and it had low competition because nobody watched it. Pick a niche you can sustain interest in and that has advertisers. Then check competition.

"Automated" affiliate sites

Generate a hundred articles, add affiliate links, wait. Google's spam policies now explicitly name "scaled content abuse", and its helpful-content guidance rewards demonstrated experience and a named author. Sites with no author, no original angle and no reason to exist are the ones publishers report losing all traffic in the 2024 and 2025 updates. This site is deliberately the opposite of that.

Reselling AI services with no skill behind them

Offering "AI logo design" or "AI copywriting" on gig sites at bottom prices. There is a race to the bottom and the bottom is very low. The sellers earning on those platforms with AI are the ones who already had the design or writing skill and use AI to deliver faster.

The pattern

Look at the two lists again. Everything that tends to work involves a real skill, a real audience or a real client. Everything that tends to fail assumes the tool will substitute for one of those.

The tools are not the business. They make the business cheaper to run. That is still a big deal, and it is the reason this site exists. But if a video tells you the tool is the business, close the video.

If you are starting from nothing

  1. Pick one of the first three methods. Writing is the fastest to first income. YouTube and Pinterest take months but compound.
  2. Start on free tiers. Claude, Canva and Perplexity are all usable for free. Pay when the free tier is the thing slowing you down, not before.
  3. Track your hours. Most people who do the maths discover their "passive" income pays a low hourly rate. That is fine if it grows. It is not fine if it does not.
  4. Read the review before you subscribe. That is what the directory is for.
