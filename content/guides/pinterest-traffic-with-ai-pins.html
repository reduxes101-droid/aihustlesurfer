Pinterest describes itself as a visual discovery engine, not a social network. Pins surface in search for months, and Pinterest's own creator guidance stresses that new accounts can get distribution without followers. For bloggers it remains one of the few sources of free traffic that is still growing.

It is also a lot of image production, which is where AI tools earn their place. This is the workflow that Pinterest-focused publishers most consistently describe, checked against Pinterest's published best practices.

What Pinterest is for

Traffic. Pinterest itself pays almost nothing. The pin links to a page you own, and that page earns through affiliate links, ads, a product, or a newsletter. If you do not have a page worth landing on, make that first. A beautiful pin to a thin article is a wasted pin.

What to expect, so you can calibrate

  • Month one: publishers commonly report a few hundred impressions a day and single-digit clicks.
  • Around month three: the first pins that "take", and impressions climbing into the thousands a day for an account that has been consistent.
  • Month five and beyond: outbound clicks in the low thousands a month is a frequently reported milestone for a small account pinning around 15 fresh pins a week.

Pinterest is slow to start and then compounds. Old pins keep working. That is the reason to do it and the reason to be patient.

The pin itself

A pin that gets saved has three things: an image that stops the scroll, a title that promises something specific, and a size that fits the feed. Everything else is secondary.

  • Size: Pinterest's documentation recommends a 2:3 aspect ratio, 1000 by 1500 pixels. Taller pins may be cropped in the feed.
  • Text on the image: yes, large, readable at thumbnail size. Pinterest's own guidance recommends text overlay, and most saves happen without the description being read.
  • Consistency: pins on the same board should look like they belong together. Accounts that look like one person made them are widely reported to outperform accounts that look like a template library.

The workflow

The video shows it end to end. In writing:

1. Background images in Midjourney

Midjourney generates the image. Do not ask it for text; it misspells often enough to waste credits, which is the most common complaint in its user community. Use a prompt template per board, a style reference image to keep the look consistent, and aspect ratio 2:3. Twenty backgrounds takes around fifteen minutes on the Standard plan in relaxed mode.

A style per niche works better than a style per pin. Clean flat-lay photography with warm light suits finance and productivity boards; bright interiors with one strong colour suit home boards. Test a few styles for two weeks and keep the one with the best save rate.

2. Text in Canva

Two saved templates per account. Drop the background in, type the title, export. Canva's Bulk Create does this from a spreadsheet: forty titles in, forty pins out, in a couple of minutes. This is the step that turns pin production from an hour a day into an hour a week.

Titles are written by a person, in a spreadsheet, in batches. AI-suggested pin titles are consistently vague. "10 budgeting tips" loses to "the $50 grocery week that actually worked" every time.

3. Descriptions and keywords

Claude writes descriptions from the title and the article, instructed to include two natural keywords and no hashtags. Pinterest's guidance says descriptions are indexed for search, so keywords belong there. Then read them. Two minutes per batch.

4. Scheduling

Canva's scheduler or Pinterest's own. Two to three pins a day, spread out, to relevant boards. Pinterest has said publicly that it prioritises fresh pins, meaning new images, over repins. Do not pin the same image to ten boards on the same day; Pinterest's spam guidance treats that pattern as low quality, and publishers report it does not work anyway.

Mistakes publishers admit to

  • Too many boards too early. Ten boards with three pins each look empty. Three boards with thirty pins each look like an account worth following.
  • Trusting Midjourney with text. Credits wasted before the jobs get separated.
  • Pins to weak pages. Clicks that bounce are a signal Pinterest is widely believed to notice, and reach falls. Fix the page first.
  • Reading analytics daily. Nothing useful happens in a day. Check weekly.

Is it worth it?

If you have pages that earn per visit, yes. The workflow above costs about an hour a week per account and $45 a month in tools that are shared with everything else you do. Publishers who stick with it describe traffic that keeps arriving for pins made months earlier.

If you do not have pages that earn, Pinterest is a way to send free traffic to nothing. Build the page, then come back.
